# Positioning and messaging

Requires: nothing to install. It works from the words and facts the user supplies.

Positioning is a set of decisions, not a slogan. If the page says nothing distinctive, rewriting it will not help, so this
skill comes first. I would rather the output said "you have no proof for that claim" than flatter the product.

## Inputs

- Real customer words: reviews, emails, call notes, survey answers. Ask for at least ten phrases verbatim. Do not invent any.
- What customers do today instead, including doing nothing and using a spreadsheet.
- What the product does that the alternatives do not, and the evidence for each point.
- Who buys, who uses and who decides, if they differ.

## Steps

1. List the alternatives: direct competitors, indirect ones and the status quo. Mark which one the best-fit buyer
   actually uses today.
2. Write the unique attributes as plain facts ("collects the bike from your door"), not adjectives ("convenient").
3. For each attribute, write the value it gives the buyer and the proof you hold. Use [the canvas](references/canvas.md).
   If there is no proof, mark it "claim without proof" and keep it out of the copy.
4. Pick the best-fit customer: the group that gets the most value, buys fastest and stays. Name them in their own terms.
5. Name the category the buyer would search for, so people know what the thing is before they hear why it is better.
6. Write the statement: "For [customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key value]. Unlike [alternative], it [difference], shown by [proof]."
7. Build the message hierarchy: one headline claim, three supporting points each with proof, then the top five objections with honest answers.
8. Extract a vocabulary list from the customer words: phrases to use and jargon to avoid. Cite which source each phrase came from.
9. Propose a five-second test the user can run: show the headline to five people in the target group and ask what it is and who it is for.

## Output

The filled canvas; the statement; the message hierarchy; the claims table (claim, proof, where it lives, usable yes or no);
the vocabulary list with sources; the objections and answers; and the test to run.

## Exit criteria

- [ ] The statement names the customer, the need, the category, the alternative, the difference and the proof.
- [ ] Every claim in the hierarchy has proof the user can show, or is marked unusable.
- [ ] The vocabulary list has at least eight phrases taken verbatim from supplied material, with their sources, or states none were supplied.
- [ ] There is one headline claim, not three competing ones.
- [ ] The best-fit customer is a group someone could find, not "everyone".

## Edge cases and errors

- The user has no customers yet: use interviews or the problem as described by people in the target group; label everything a hypothesis.
- Several products or segments: position one at a time, starting with the one that earns most.
- The difference is only price: say so and check it is sustainable. Do not dress it up.
- The user insists on a claim without proof: record it as "unproven" and keep it out of headlines.
- A claim you cannot verify: ask for the source. Never fill the gap with a plausible number.
- The proof is a customer quote the user cannot show: ask for permission or leave it out.

## Rules

- Customer language beats company language.
- Facts, not adjectives. "Fast" is not a difference; "collected and returned the same day" is.
- No invented testimonials, numbers or client names.
- Do not promise results the product cannot show.
